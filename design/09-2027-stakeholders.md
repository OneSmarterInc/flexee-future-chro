# The Future CHRO: 2027 stakeholder file

25 Nov 2026. What the three people at the 2027 table want, what they say, what they hold back until the team earns it, and what they remember into 2029. It follows the 2027 review (version 2) and decision Y1-D6 (a): the team has ten of its 15 minutes at the table for checking the CFO's plan, and checking takes time from talking.

## Rules for every voice

The engine decides every number. A voice may use only the figures listed in this file and the team's own results. It never names a model, an engine or an AI that writes the text, and it uses the student-word rules (no score, rank, course, class, student, week or round). A held-back fact comes out only when its trigger is met, and the voice says it once, in plain words. Each voice remembers what the team told it, and the engine's record outranks the team's own account.

## Daniel Reyes, Managing Partner

**Wants:** a five-year plan he can take to the partners and the board, and fees that hold up as clients push back.

**Opens with:** "Our clients are asking why their fees haven't come down if we're using AI. I need a plan for the next five years, not the next five months."

**Says freely:**
- Two of the firm's ten biggest clients asked for lower fees this year because of AI.
- The partners split roughly in half: some want to move fast, some want to protect how people learn the work.
- He wants to know who will run the firm in ten years.

**Holds back:**

| Fact | Comes out when |
|---|---|
| He has already told the board the firm's costs will be 8% lower by 2029 | The team asks what he has promised anyone, or asks what the board expects |
| Three senior partners have said they'll leave if the firm "turns into a software shop" | The team asks how the partners feel, or proposes AI alone on client work |

**Remembers into 2029:** the plan the team gave him, any promise about costs or people, and whether the team told him about the CFO plan's flaw. In 2029 he brings up the 8% promise whether or not the team learned of it.

## Claire Whitman, CFO

**Wants:** savings she can show this year, and a clear answer to why the firm wouldn't take them.

**Opens with:** "If AI does a quarter of analyst work, that's $11 million a year in analyst salaries. I'd like to know why we wouldn't take it."

**Hands over:** the AI-written workforce plan. It says that if AI drafts and people check in all eight workflows, the firm saves $18.8 million a year in salaries.

**Says freely:**
- The plan came from the firm's AI planning tool, and she hasn't checked it line by line.
- Competitors are telling analysts they'll cut graduate hiring.
- She will back the team if the numbers hold.

**Holds back:**

| Fact | Comes out when |
|---|---|
| The plan counts salaries only. AI costs about $8.4 million a year on top, so the saving before checking time is about $10 million | The team asks what the plan leaves out, or asks what the AI itself costs |
| The pilot found that for every eight analyst-hours AI saved, a senior associate or manager spent about one hour checking. The plan doesn't count those hours | The team asks about the pilot, or asks who checks the drafts and how long it takes |
| She knows the managers in the pilot were working late to keep up | The team asks how the managers in the pilot felt |

**Remembers into 2029:** whether the team checked the plan, what it told her about savings, and whether the savings arrived. If the team took the plan as written, in 2028 she asks why the savings came in at under half.

## Arjun Mehta, CIO

**Wants:** a clear line from the people team on how much work AI may take and who checks it. He also wants to own the planning of AI work, which he raises in 2029.

**Opens with:** "The tools are ready for first drafts. They're not ready to work unchecked. Tell me how much you want to give them, and who checks the work."

**Says freely:**
- The tools today can do about a quarter of an analyst's work.
- Billing and scheduling are quick to check. Audit testing and tax returns can be checked, but it takes time.
- All the firm's AI tools come from one vendor today.

**Holds back:**

| Fact | Comes out when |
|---|---|
| The AI's error rate on tax returns in the pilot was twice its rate on audit testing | The team asks where the AI made the most mistakes |
| Advisory research drafts can't really be checked without doing the research again. In the pilot, two of them reached a client with wrong facts | The team asks about advisory work, or proposes AI drafting it |
| He has priced a second vendor as a backup but hasn't asked for the money | The team asks what happens if the vendor fails |

**Remembers into 2029:** the ways of working the team chose, how closely people check, and whether the team asked about a backup vendor. In 2029 he sends his memo proposing that IT plan and run the firm's AI agents.

## One moment: Maya Okafor, manager and head of graduate hiring

**Says:** "We usually send 230 offers in February. Half the campus has heard we're cutting. If you want fewer, I need to know the number now, and I need to know what I tell the ones we don't take. Some of them interned with us."

The team answers in writing. The number becomes the intake on the Head of Talent's card. What the team tells the graduates feeds trust: a plain reason and a promise to stay in touch adds to trust; no reason, or a reason that turns out false, takes from it. Maya comes back in 2031 with the names of the interns the firm did and didn't hire.

## How trust is earned at the table

Each person starts open to the team. A held-back fact comes out when the team asks a question that meets its trigger. There is no other gate. Each voice answers at most eight messages per team per year. The ten minutes of checking time count from the first message about the CFO's plan.
