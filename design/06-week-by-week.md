# The Future CHRO: week by week

24 Nov 2026. One paragraph per week for the term format: eight weeks of play (one simulated year each, 2027 to 2034) and a ninth week for the debrief, when the 2035 results and the replay under three futures come out. The workshop uses the same eight years at about 45 minutes each. Each paragraph names the model mechanism it rests on. Weeks 1 to 5 rest on mechanisms in model v0.2. Weeks 6 to 8 lean more on story and are marked for review.

## Week 1 (2027): The graduate class

The team meets Ashby Calder: 2,006 people, $389 million in fees, a 25% margin, and AI tools that can already do a quarter of an analyst's work at less than half the cost. The Managing Partner wants a five-year plan, the CFO sees $11 million in analyst salaries, and the CIO says the tools are ready for drafts but not for unchecked work. Maya Okafor needs to know how many of the usual 230 graduate offers go out in February, and what to tell the interns who don't get one. The team sets the way of working for each of its eight workflows, the intake, the coaching design and how closely people check AI. Nobody who adopts AI makes more money this year than a firm that changes nothing, and the lesson is that a decision about entry-level work is a decision about who leads the firm in ten years. (Mechanisms: workflows, intake, tutor, oversight, the J-curve.)

## Week 2 (2028): The hours nobody counted

The savings arrive, but smaller than the CFO's figure. Senior associates and managers are spending part of every week checking AI drafts, and that time came out of client work and out of teaching. A manager in tax, the team's best reviewer, asks to step back because she's reviewing more than she's thinking. The team decides how to share what AI saves, whether to check every piece of work or only samples, and whether to move any workflow back to people drafting first. A signal arrives this year: most staff say AI makes them faster, but few firms see it in profit. The lesson is that AI's cost includes the hours of the people who check it, and those hours fall on the people the firm can least spare. (Mechanisms: review load by task type, strain, gain sharing.)

## Week 3 (2029): Who owns the digital workforce

Arjun Mehta, the CIO, sends the Managing Partner a memo proposing that IT plan and run the firm's AI agents as a "digital workforce". The board chair and the employee council join the table for the first time, and the board asks for a report on people and skills. The team decides who plans AI work (HR, IT or both), where the firm's knowledge will be kept (in people, in a firm-owned system or with the AI vendor), and whether to report to the board each year. A regulator says people can't watch every agent decision in real time, so checking has to be aimed. The team can now spend on checking capacity: reviewer training and checking tools. The futures start to look different now, and the signals are louder and still mixed. The lesson is that the CHRO keeps a seat at the top table by owning the hard questions about AI and people, not by giving them up. (Mechanisms: planning authority, influence, knowledge stock, board report.)

## Week 4 (2030): The outage

For three weeks in spring the firm's main AI vendor fails, and every workflow that depended on it slows or stops. Firms with people who still know the work, and with spare capacity, recover in days. Firms that let AI work alone on that vendor lose the most, because the failure spreads through every workflow it runs. They lose a big part of a quarter's output and miss client deadlines. A partner calls about an audit opinion that will be late. The team decides how much human backup to keep, which workflows may run on AI alone, and how to talk to clients. The lesson is that resilience is a people decision made years before the failure. (Mechanisms: the 2030 outage, ways of working, spare capacity.)

## Week 5 (2031): The missing seniors and the new contract

The graduates who weren't hired in 2027 are the senior associates the firm doesn't have now. Promotion lists are thin, managers are stretched, and outside hires cost 60% more and are scarce if competitors cut too. In the same year the AI vendor changes its terms: higher prices, and a firm that kept its knowledge in the vendor's tools loses part of it. A partner asks the team directly who will run her engagements in three years. The team decides on outside hiring, promotion rules, mentoring time and whether to bring its knowledge back in-house. The lesson is the core of the sim: the cost of the 2027 choice arrives in 2031. (Mechanisms: pipeline strength, lateral market, vendor terms, knowledge.)

## Week 6 (2032): The expert who wants to leave early (to review)

A senior tax partner with 25 years of cases wants to retire two years early. She is tired, and she says the work has become checking machines instead of judging problems. Whether her knowledge stays depends on choices the team made in weeks 3 and 5. The team decides on retirement terms, knowledge capture, how experts spend their time, and whether to redesign senior roles around judgment and teaching. The lesson is that expertise is an asset that wears away when it isn't used and leaves when people do. (Mechanisms: retirement, knowledge capture, strain at senior levels. The early-retirement offer itself needs a small model addition.)

## Week 7 (2033): The future shows itself (to review)

In the rapid future this is the year of the verification wall: AI output has grown so much that the seniors left can't check it, and errors more than double. By now the drawn future is hard to miss: prices have fallen hard, or AI has hit a ceiling, or the law has tightened. A team leader in operations is replaced by an AI agent that schedules and routes work, and her team asks what happens to them. The board asks whether the firm's plan still fits the world. The team decides whether to change direction, on layoffs or redeployment, and on what it tells people. The lesson is that changing course when the evidence arrives is part of judgment, and so is how you treat the people the change affects. (Mechanisms: price index by future, layoffs, trust, transparency. The team-leader story needs AI work at level 2 in operations, which the model has.)

## Week 8 (2034): What you leave behind (to review)

It's the last year of play. The Managing Partner asks the team to name the people who could lead the firm in 2040 and to write a one-page handover to the next CHRO. The team makes its final decisions on intake, development and governance, knowing the results will be read in 2035. The lesson is that the CHRO's real output is the firm's future capability, and that can only be judged after you've gone. (Mechanisms: pipeline strength, expert and leader bench, the yearly paragraph as the handover.)

## Week 9: Debrief, the three futures

The team sees its 2035 results in the future that happened, and then sees the same decisions replayed under the two futures that didn't. A plan that holds up in all three shows judgment. A plan that won only in the future that arrived shows luck. The class compares teams on both, reads its own yearly paragraphs against what happened, and discusses which signals they read correctly and which they missed. The judgment score is the team's worst result across the three futures. (Mechanisms: replay, judgment score, S1 weights.)
