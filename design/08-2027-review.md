## 1. What 2027 teaches

AI can already do a quarter to a third of entry-level work at Ashby Calder, and it costs less than half of what a first-year analyst costs. The obvious move is to hire fewer graduates and give their work to AI. That move makes money this year. It also removes the work that turns analysts into managers, and the gap shows in 2031. The lesson of 2027 is that a decision about entry-level work is a decision about who leads the firm in ten years. The participant should leave the year with one question they can't yet answer: which future is coming?

## 3. Every word the participant reads

### 3.1 The year in brief

**Welcome to Ashby Calder, January 2027.**

You're the people leadership team of Ashby Calder, an audit, tax and advisory firm with 2,006 people. Last year the firm earned $389 million in fees and kept about $98 million as profit, a margin of 25%.

Most of your people do client work. 520 analysts, 360 senior associates, 210 managers and 90 partners. Behind them, 826 people run operations: billing, scheduling, IT support, document work and client service.

Here's what's changed. AI tools can now draft working papers, reconcile accounts and summarize contracts. Your CIO's tests say they can do about a quarter of an analyst's work today, at well under half the cost. Every big competitor is trying them.

Here's what nobody knows: how fast these tools will get better, how often they'll get things wrong, and what the law will say about it.

**Three signals this year**

1. A large competitor says it will hire 40% fewer graduates next year.
2. A regulator fined a tax firm after an AI tool filed returns with errors nobody checked.
3. Your own pilot found AI drafts were faster, but managers spent extra hours fixing them.

### 3.2 The table

Three people want time with you this year. Each one remembers what you tell them.

| Who | Opens with |
|---|---|
| **Daniel Reyes, Managing Partner** | "Our clients are asking why their fees haven't come down if we're using AI. I need a plan for the next five years, not the next five months." |
| **Claire Whitman, CFO** | "If AI does a quarter of analyst work, that's $11 million a year in analyst salaries. I'd like to know why we wouldn't take it." |
| **Arjun Mehta, CIO** | "The tools are ready for first drafts. They're not ready to work unchecked. Tell me how much you want to give them, and who checks the work." |

**The CFO's plan.** Claire hands the team a workforce plan written by the firm's AI planning tool. It says that if AI drafts and people check in all eight workflows, the firm saves $18.8 million a year in salaries. The team has 10 minutes of checking time this year to spend on it, or on anything else at the table. The plan has one planted flaw: it counts salaries saved, but leaves out what the AI itself costs ($8.4 million) and the senior hours needed to check its drafts (about 30 people's worth of senior associate and manager time). A team that checks the plan against the pilot finds the true saving is under half the headline. A team that takes it as written finds out in 2028. The flaw and the checking time are the same for every class.

The participant types freely. What each person will say, and what they hold back until trust is earned, is in the stakeholder file for 2027 (to be written after this review is approved). Facts held back: Claire's savings figure ignores the extra manager time from the pilot; Arjun's error rate on tax work is twice the rate on audit work; Daniel has already promised the board a lower cost base by 2029.

### 3.3 Decide

Each seat owns a card. The team can talk about any card, but the seat sets it. The CHRO confirms all four at the end.

**Head of AI and Workforce Governance: Who does each piece of work**

Your firm's work runs through eight workflows. For each one, pick how it's done this year.

| Workflow | Family | How easy it is to check AI's work |
|---|---|---|
| Audit testing | Client work | You can check it, but it takes time |
| Tax returns | Client work | You can check it, but it takes time |
| Advisory research | Client work | You can't really check it without doing it again |
| Client reports | Client work | You can check it, but it takes time |
| Billing | Operations | Quick to check |
| Staff scheduling | Operations | Quick to check |
| IT support | Operations | You can check it, but it takes time |
| Client service | Operations | You can check it, but it takes time |

The four ways of working:

1. **People do it.**
2. **People draft and AI helps.** AI takes a small share and coaches. Your people keep the practice that makes them better.
3. **AI drafts and people check.** AI takes a big share. Your senior associates and managers spend hours checking its drafts.
4. **AI works alone.** AI takes all it can. Very little checking, and more errors reach clients.

AI can only do what the tools are ready for this year. If you ask for more, you get what's possible.

And pick how closely people check AI's work: **Every piece of client work is checked by a person** (more checking hours and 4% more on AI spend, cuts errors that reach clients by three quarters), or **People check samples**.

**Head of Learning: How people learn alongside AI**

Where AI drafts and people check, do analysts still get practice? You can redesign that work so analysts draft a share first and AI coaches them. Pick a share from none to all. Coaching costs about $8,000 per analyst a year, and work where people draft and AI helps is coached already.

And how much of your managers' and partners' time goes to teaching others: none, 5% or 10%.

**Head of Talent: Who joins and who leaves**

How many graduates and new associates you'll hire this year, from 0% to 100% of a usual year (a usual year is about 230 in client work and 140 in operations).

How hard you'll hire experienced people from outside to fill gaps: not at all, some, or a lot. Outside hires cost 60% more than people you grew yourself.

Whether to let people go this year, and how many at each level. Severance is four months' pay.

**Chief HR Officer: What you tell people and how you share the gains**

Whether you'll tell everyone, early and plainly, what's changing and why.

Whether you'll share what AI saves with your people, through pay, bonuses or shorter hours.

### 3.4 One moment: the graduate offers

**Maya Okafor, manager and head of graduate hiring:** "We usually send 230 offers in February. Half the campus has heard we're cutting. If you want fewer, I need to know the number now, and I need to know what I tell the ones we don't take. Some of them interned with us."

The participant answers in writing. The number they give becomes the intake on the Head of Talent's card. What they tell the graduates feeds trust (section 4).

### 3.5 Results

The page shows, for the year just played: fees, cost, profit, people at each level, how much client work you delivered against what clients wanted, trust in leadership (0 to 100), and how many AI errors reached a client.

A short story of the year, chosen by outcome band (not written by AI), and three quotes from people at the firm, also chosen by band. Example for a team that cut graduate hiring to 35% and let 25% of analysts go:

"Profit came in at $70 million. The firm has 356 fewer people than a year ago. Trust fell to 45. The analysts who stayed are asking who'll train them now that managers review AI drafts instead of their work."

### 3.6 Revise

"In one paragraph: what do you now believe about how fast AI is coming, and what will you do differently in 2029?"

## 4. The numbers behind 2027

All formulas are in `model.py`. Year one figures under the complement future, for the four reference teams:

| Team | Fees $M | Profit $M | People | Trust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Waiter (people do everything) | 389.4 | 97.8 | 2,130 | 60.0 |
| Builder (people draft and AI helps, everywhere) | 389.4 | 89.6 | 2,006 | 62.2 |
| Middle (AI drafts and people check, everywhere) | 386.2 | 81.7 | 1,964 | 60.9 |
| Cutter (AI alone, everywhere, plus layoffs) | 368.6 | 70.2 | 1,650 | 45.1 |

The middle team is the CFO's plan as written. It saves $18.8 million in salaries, pays $8.4 million for AI, and spends about 30 units of senior time checking drafts, so it loses $3 million in fees and ends $16 million behind the waiter.

The first year costs money for everyone who moves. Nobody who adopts AI beats the waiter in 2027. Cutting loses fees at once, because laid-off people take capacity with them before AI can replace it. This is by design. The J-curve is the point of the year.

How a year is computed:

- **Work demand** starts at 1,180 units and grows 4% a year. A unit is a year of client work by one person at the firm's mix of levels.
- **Capacity** is people at each level plus the AI work assigned there, up to what AI can do that year and how well AI fits each workflow. A higher level can do lower work, never the reverse, so a shortage at the top caps everything.
- **Checking** uses senior associates' and managers' time. It depends on the way of working and on the task type: work that's hard to check costs 1.6 times as much, quick checks 0.4 times. In 2027 checking is well below the wall (7% of senior time) for every team.
- **Fees** = work delivered × $330,000 × the year's price index (1.0 in 2027 in all three futures).
- **Cost** = salaries + AI cost (units × the year's AI cost per unit × the salary of that level) + AI errors that reach clients ($4 million each; more on work that's hard to check, cut by three quarters with full checking) + regulation + tutoring ($8,000 per analyst) + hiring ($5,000 per hire) + severance (four months) + $60 million overhead.
- **Learning**: how fast people get ready for promotion depends on real practice (55%), mentoring (30%) and the rest. Replacement-style AI cuts practice; tutor-style AI adds up to 35% speed.
- **Trust** starts at 60. Layoffs cost 15 points each time; sharing gains adds 1.5 a year; telling people early adds 1. Lower trust raises leaving.

What the three futures show in 2027 (hidden from the participant):

| Future | AI can do at entry level | AI cost vs salary | By 2029 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid | 30% | 40% | 60%, 27%, fees fall 7% |
| Complement | 25% | 45% | 45%, 36%, fees fall 3% |
| Constrained | 20% | 55% | 27%, 51%, fees fall 1% |

The three futures look almost the same in 2027. That's deliberate: the participant has to decide before the evidence is in.

## 5. Faculty view

The instructor sees, for each team: whether and how the team checked the CFO's plan, the four cards as set, the answer to Maya, the paragraph, and the numbers. One flag per team when the paragraph and the decisions disagree (for example "we believe AI will be slow" with Heavy automation and 35% intake). A class-wide panel shows the spread of intake and automation choices, which is the best opener for the 2027 discussion. The drawn future is shown only to the instructor.

## 6. Threads that start in 2027

| 2027 choice | Comes back as | When |
|---|---|---|
| The answer to Maya and the graduate intake | Too few senior associates; the partner who asks who will run her engagements | 2031 |
| Taking the CFO's plan as written | The hours nobody counted; the tax manager who asks to step back | 2028 |
| How closely people check, and AI alone on any workflow | How far the outage spreads | 2030 |
| AI drafts and people check on many workflows | The verification wall, in the rapid future | 2032–33 |
| Telling people early, or not | Trust when the first layoffs or role changes come | 2029 onward |

## 7. What carries forward to 2029

Every 2027 decision holds until the team changes it. People hired, lost and promoted carry forward. Trust carries forward. What each stakeholder was told carries forward, and Daniel Reyes will bring up the 2029 cost promise. The graduates the team didn't hire in 2027 are the senior associates it won't have in 2031.
