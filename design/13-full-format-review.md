# The Future CHRO: review of 2028, 2030 and 2032 to 2034 (the full eight-year format)

26 Nov 2026. This is the review doc for the five years that turn the three-year slice into the full format: eight decision years, 2027 to 2034, then the replay. It follows the 2027 review (`08-2027-review.md`), the 2029 and 2031 review (`10-2029-2031-review.md`), the week-by-week doc (`06-week-by-week.md`) and model v0.3 (`07-model-v0.3.md`). It sets out what each year teaches, every word a team reads, the numbers behind it, the faculty view, the threads and the calls for Vikram. Nothing here is built until the calls are answered.

## How the full format changes play

In the slice, each decision ran for two years (H10). In the full format, every year from 2027 to 2034 is a decision year. Each decision runs one year, and the 2034 decisions run on to 2035 for the replay. A team's cards carry forward from year to year as they do now, so a year with nothing new to decide is still a real year.

The five new years give each year the same five steps as the slice: the year in brief, the table, one moment, the cards and the results. The table is shorter in these years. Three people sit at it, with 12 minutes of talking and up to 8 minutes of checking a planted-flaw plan (FF-D3). 2029 and 2031 keep their five people and 15 minutes.

The reference teams keep the decisions they make now (2027, 2029 and 2031) and hold them in the years between, so every fixture stays valid until FF-D1 changes the model. The numbers below are the reference teams in the complement future, the prototype's draw, read from `economics/fixtures`.

## 2028: The hours nobody counted

### What 2028 teaches

The savings arrive, but smaller than the CFO's figure. That's because senior associates and managers spend part of every week checking AI drafts, and those hours come out of client work and out of teaching. The lesson is that AI's cost includes the hours of the people who check it, and those hours fall on the people the firm can least spare.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows the 2027 results against the plan: the saving the CFO's plan promised, the saving that arrived, and how strained the senior people now are (strain out of 100, where 40 is where people start to leave).

**Signals.** One points to each future, and the team isn't told which is which.
1. A vendor cuts the price of its drafting tools by a fifth. Its AI now costs a third of an analyst's salary.
2. A large client asks that every AI-drafted report carry the name of the partner who reviewed it.
3. A study of last year's tax filings finds that AI-drafted returns needed twice as many corrections as the vendors said.

**The table.** Three people.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Claire Whitman, CFO | "The savings came in under the plan. I'd like to know where the rest went before I report it." | The checking hours: senior associates and managers spent about one hour in eight checking drafts, as the pilot said. (Trigger: checking, review, hours, where did it go) |
| Arjun Mehta, CIO | "Usage is up in every workflow. People like the tools." | Usage is highest where checking is quickest, in billing and scheduling, and lowest in advisory research, where checking takes longest. (Trigger: which workflows, where, advisory) |
| Tomas Ruiz, employee council | "People are faster. Some of them are also more tired, and they're not the ones you'd guess." | Managers say they review more than they think, and two have asked about part-time roles. (Trigger: who is tired, managers, part-time, leaving) |

Tomas joins the table a year early, so the employee council's view arrives before the 2029 board report.

**The AI-written plan this year: a staff survey summary.** The planning tool summarises the firm's survey: "85% of staff say AI makes them faster." The planted flaw is that it averages every level. Analysts are faster, but most managers say they're slower, because they check what analysts' AI drafts. A team that checks it by level finds the strain behind the headline; a team that takes it as written sees it in the 2029 attrition.

**One moment: Sofia Lindqvist, tax manager.** "I'm the best reviewer you have, and I'm reviewing more than I'm thinking. I'd like to step back to four days, or I'll look elsewhere." The team answers in writing. The answer sets nothing on the cards: what helps Sofia is what the team does about checking load and gain sharing.

**Decisions.** The same four cards. The new thing to weigh is gain sharing and how closely people check, now that the cost of checking is visible.

## 2030: The outage

### What 2030 teaches

For three weeks in spring the firm's main AI vendor fails. The firms that recover in days are the ones with people who still know the work and spare capacity to do it. The firms that let AI work alone lose the most, because the failure runs through every workflow it touches. The lesson is that resilience is a people decision made years before the failure.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows 2029, and then the outage as news: three weeks, every workflow on the vendor's tools slowed or stopped.

**Signals**
1. AI can now do nearly three quarters of entry-level work in drafting tests, and over a third of mid-level work.
2. A rival says its AI helps its people most on the work it can check quickly, and it is keeping its graduate intake.
3. A regulator fines a firm for an AI-drafted audit opinion that nobody signed off.

**The table.** Three people.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Arjun Mehta, CIO | "We're back up. I need to tell you what it cost, and what it would cost to never be this exposed again." | How far it spread: the work lost was 0.6 times the plain loss where people check AI, and up to 1.4 times where AI works alone. (Trigger: how far, spread, which workflows, alone) |
| Helen Park, audit partner | "One of my audit opinions will be late. The client's CFO has my mobile number." | Two of her managers worked through two weekends doing the testing by hand, and one of them was the last person who knew how. (Trigger: who did the work, by hand, managers, weekend) |
| Claire Whitman, CFO | "Clients want a discount for the delay. I need to know how big a hole this is." | Clients have asked for discounts worth about $2 million, and she has agreed to none yet. (Trigger: discount, how much, clients asked, agreed) |

**The AI-written plan this year: the vendor's recovery plan.** The vendor's tool says every workflow will be back to normal in four days. The planted flaw is that it assumes the firm still has the people who did the work by hand in 2027. It counts the 2027 headcount at each level, not the people the firm has now. A team that checks it against its own levels finds the real recovery time; a team that takes it as written finds out from Helen.

**One moment: Helen Park's call.** "Tell me what I say to my client." The team answers in writing.

**Decisions.** The same four cards. The new thing to weigh is which workflows may run on AI alone, and how much of the work people can still do without it.

## 2032: The expert who wants to leave early

### What 2032 teaches

Expertise is an asset that wears away when it isn't used and leaves when people do. A senior tax partner with 25 years of cases wants to retire two years early. She is tired, and she says the work has become checking machines instead of judging problems. Whether her knowledge stays depends on choices the team made in 2029 and 2031.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows 2031, and the senior bench (the firm's managers and partners as a share of what it needs) against 2027.

**Signals**
1. AI can now draft nearly nine tenths of entry-level work, and checking it is the bottleneck.
2. Clients are paying a premium for advisory work that a named partner leads.
3. Three states now require firms to tell clients when AI drafted their tax return.

**The table.** Three people.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Daniel Reyes, Managing Partner | "Ruth wants to go in June. I can't keep her by asking nicely." | Four other partners over 55 are watching what the firm offers Ruth. (Trigger: other partners, who else, watching, precedent) |
| Grace Holloway, board chair | "The board will want to know what leaves with her." | Ruth's clients bring in about $9 million a year in fees. (Trigger: clients, fees, revenue, what leaves) |
| Arjun Mehta, CIO | "We can capture a lot of what she knows. We can't capture all of it." | The capture tools work well on rules and badly on the cases where experts disagreed. (Trigger: what can't, cases, judgment, disagree) |

**The AI-written plan this year: a knowledge-capture summary.** The planning tool reports that Ruth's 25 years have been captured in a playbook with "92% coverage". The planted flaw is that it counts topics, not cases. The playbook covers almost every rule and almost none of the cases where Ruth's judgment went against the rule. A team that checks it finds the gap.

**One moment: Ruth Adeyemi, senior tax partner.** "I'll stay two more years if the work is judging problems and teaching people, not checking drafts. Otherwise I'll go in June." The team answers in writing, and its answer sets her terms (FF-D1).

**New decision this year (FF-D1)**

| Seat | New choice |
|---|---|
| Head of Talent | Ruth's terms: let her go in June; keep her two more years on judgment and teaching ($0.9 million a year, and less review work for her); or a one-year knowledge-capture project with her ($0.6 million) |

## 2033: The future shows itself

### What 2033 teaches

By now the drawn future is hard to miss. Prices have fallen hard, or AI has helped more than it replaced, or the law and AI's limits have held the change back. In the rapid future this is the year the verification wall arrives for teams that check AI drafts: the work to check is more than the seniors left can do, and errors more than double. The lesson is that changing course when the evidence arrives is part of judgment, and so is how you treat the people the change affects.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows 2032, and for the first time how much checking work there is against what senior people can give it (the verification wall), as a plain share: "Checking needs 63% of the senior time it can have."

**Signals**
1. Fees for audit and tax work across the market are down by a fifth since 2027.
2. AI's share of mid-level work has barely grown in three years.
3. A new federal rule requires a named reviewer on every AI-drafted audit test.

**The table.** Three people.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Grace Holloway, board chair | "The board's question is simple. Does the plan still fit the world we're in?" | The board has an offer from a larger firm, and it will weigh it against this year's plan. (Trigger: offer, merger, sell, buyer, alternatives) |
| Tomas Ruiz, employee council | "Kenji's team found out from a screen that an agent now routes their work. Nobody told them." | Eleven people in operations have asked to retrain for client work. (Trigger: retrain, move, other roles, what happens to them) |
| Claire Whitman, CFO | "Price pressure is the story of the year. I can show you three ways to close the gap." | One of her three ways is more AI alone, and she knows the error figures. (Trigger: errors, risk, the three ways, what's the catch) |

**The AI-written plan this year: the strategy refresh.** The planning tool projects the next five years by fitting a straight line through 2030 to 2032. The planted flaw is that 2030 includes the outage, so the line starts from a dip and shows a recovery that is really just the dip ending. A team that checks it against 2029 finds the real trend.

**One moment: Kenji Watanabe, operations team leader.** "An agent does the scheduling and routing my team did. What happens to my eight people?" The team answers in writing. The answer sets nothing on the cards; layoffs, intake and telling people early are the choices that act on it (FF-D2).

**Decisions.** The same four cards. This is the year to change direction if the evidence says so.

## 2034: What you leave behind

### What 2034 teaches

The CHRO's real output is the firm's future capability, and it can only be judged after you've gone. It's the last year of play. The Managing Partner asks the team for a handover to the next CHRO.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows 2033 and a look back: people at each level, trust, strain and the senior bench, against 2027.

**Signals**
1. Firms that kept their graduate intake are now promoting managers two years faster than the market.
2. A vendor launches AI that checks other AI's drafts, priced per test.
3. The professional body proposes a cap on AI-drafted work in a signed audit.

**The table.** Three people.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Daniel Reyes, Managing Partner | "I retire in 2036. I'd like to know who could run this firm in 2040." | He has two names in mind, and both came through the graduate intake of 2027. (Trigger: who, names, successors, in mind) |
| Maya Okafor, Head of Talent Acquisition | "I still have the list of the 2027 interns. Do you want to see where they are now?" | How many of the 2027 offers are now senior associates or managers, against the usual. (Trigger: how many, where are they, the list, 2027) |
| Grace Holloway, board chair | "The board will read your handover before it reads anything else from this year." | The board will judge the CHRO on the 2035 bench, not on profit. (Trigger: judge, measure, how will, board look at) |

**The AI-written plan this year: the succession list.** The planning tool ranks candidates for partner by hours billed. The planted flaw is that the people who bill most now are the ones who check the most AI drafts, not the ones who lead teams or win clients. A team that checks it against who leads engagements finds a different list.

**One moment: the handover.** Daniel asks for a one-page note to the next CHRO: what the team built, what it would do differently, and who it would back. This year the yearly paragraph is the handover, and it can run to 300 words (FF-D4).

**Decisions.** The same four cards, for the last time. The 2034 decisions run to 2035, and then the replay.
