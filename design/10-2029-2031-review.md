# The Future CHRO: 2029 and 2031 review, and the replay

26 Nov 2026. Review doc for the second and third playable years and the closing replay, written before they go into the prototype. It follows the 2027 review (version 2) and uses model v0.3. In the prototype each year is a two-year jump (H10): decisions made in 2029 also run 2030, and decisions made in 2031 run on to 2035 for the replay. The prototype's drawn future is complement.

## 2029: Who owns the digital workforce

### What 2029 teaches

AI's hidden cost has arrived. The savings were real but smaller, because senior people spent their hours checking drafts. Now the CIO wants to run the firm's AI agents himself, the board wants a report on people and skills, and the firm has to decide where its knowledge lives. The lesson is that the CHRO keeps a seat at the top table by owning the hard questions about AI and people.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** Two years have passed. The page shows the team's 2028 and 2029 results against its 2027 results: fees, profit, people, trust, how strained the senior people are, and AI errors that reached clients.

**Signals**
1. Most staff across the industry say AI makes them faster, but few firms see it in their profit.
2. A regulator says people can't watch every agent decision as it happens, so checking has to be aimed where the risk is.
3. A competitor that cut its graduate intake by half is now hiring experienced auditors from outside, at a premium.

**The table.** Five people this year. The board chair and the employee council join.

| Who | Opens with | Holds back until asked |
|---|---|---|
| Daniel Reyes, Managing Partner | "Two years in. The board will ask me in March whether we're on track. Are we?" | He told the board costs would be 8% lower by 2029, and they're not |
| Claire Whitman, CFO | "The savings came in, but smaller than the plan. I want to understand where the rest went." | Checking time is now the biggest cost no one budgeted |
| Arjun Mehta, CIO | "I've sent Daniel a memo. I think IT should plan and run our AI agents as a digital workforce. It's faster, and it's cheaper." | Joint planning would cost about $1 million a year, but IT alone would keep pushing work faster than people can check it |
| Grace Holloway, board chair | "The board wants a yearly report on our people and skills. Not headcount. Capability." | Two other boards she sits on were caught out by AI errors they didn't know about |
| Tomas Ruiz, employee council | "People want to know whether their jobs are changing, and whether anyone will tell them before it happens." | Senior associates who mostly check drafts are the group most likely to leave |

**The AI-written plan this year: an attrition forecast.** The firm's planning tool forecasts that leaving will fall to 9% next year because pay rose. The planted flaw: it learned from the years before AI, so it doesn't see that senior associates and managers who spend their days checking drafts leave more. A team that checks it against the strain measure finds the risk; a team that takes it as written sees it in 2031. Ten minutes of checking time, as in 2027.

**One moment: Arjun's memo.** The team answers it in writing. The answer sets who plans AI work: HR, IT or both together.

**New decisions this year**

| Seat | New choice |
|---|---|
| Head of AI and Workforce Governance | Spend on checking capacity: reviewer training and checking tools, $2.5 million a year, cutting checking time by up to 30% over two years |
| Head of Learning | Where the firm's knowledge is kept: in people, in a firm-owned system ($3 million a year) or in the AI vendor's tools ($1 million a year) |
| Chief HR Officer | Who plans AI work (from the answer to Arjun), and whether to send the board a yearly people and skills report ($0.5 million a year) |

All 2027 choices stay on their cards and can be changed.

## 2031: The missing seniors

### What 2031 teaches

The graduates the firm didn't hire in 2027 are the senior associates it doesn't have now. In the same two years, the vendor failed for three weeks (2030) and then changed its terms (2031). The lesson is the core of the sim: the cost of the 2027 choice arrives in 2031, and it arrives together with the costs of everything else that was left to one vendor.

### What the team reads

**The year in brief.** The page shows 2030 and 2031: the outage and how much work it cost, the vendor's new terms, the pipeline strength, and people at each level against 2027.

**Signals**
1. A rival firm says its AI now drafts most first-year work with very few errors.
2. Two of the big four firms say they're rebuilding their graduate intake after a shortage of seniors.
3. A new state law requires firms to tell clients when AI drafted work they're paying for.

**The table.** The same five people. Daniel asks whether the firm can still promote enough managers. Claire brings the vendor's new prices. Arjun reports how far the outage spread. Grace asks for the people and skills report if the team chose to send one. Tomas reports what people said about the last two years.

**The AI-written plan this year: a promotion-readiness assessment.** The planning tool rates 70% of senior associates ready to be managers, based on how much work they turned out. The planted flaw: it counts AI-drafted work they only checked as their own. A team that checks it finds the real share is closer to 40%.

**One moment: Helen Park, audit partner.** "I have six clients and two managers. In three years both of them will be partners or gone. Who runs my engagements?" Maya Okafor joins her with the names of the 2027 interns the firm did and didn't hire.

**Decisions.** The same four cards. No new choices; the year is about using the ones the team has.

### The numbers (complement future)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

By 2031 the builder and the middle team overtake the waiter, whose fees are being pushed down by competitors' AI. The cutter's pipeline is a third of what it was.
